A Pattern That Found Me Twice

How the golden ratio resurfaced and reshaped the way I see the world

I first encountered the golden ratio — the proportion φ ≈ 1.618 — more than twenty years ago in The Da Vinci Code. Dan Brown described it as a “divine proportion,” a hidden structure running through nature, art, and architecture, a signature of sacred harmony woven into the world. He tied it to the Fibonacci sequence (1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13…), the simple pattern that generates the spirals we see in sunflowers, pinecones, shells, and even galaxies. It was the first time something reminded me that there is often far more to life than what sits on the surface — that beneath even the most ordinary things, there can be deeper meanings, hidden structures, and explanations for what might seem banal at first glance.

I loved the idea, but once I closed the book, it faded quietly into the background of my life.

Years later, while reading Lon Milo DuQuette’s The Chicken Qabalah, something stirred that I didn’t expect. The book never mentions the golden ratio or the Fibonacci sequence, but it does explore the idea that reality has layers beneath the surface. DuQuette introduces three classic Qabalistic techniques — Gematria, Notariqon, and Temurah — each designed to reveal hidden meaning inside ordinary words. Gematria links words through shared numerical values. Notariqon expands or compresses a word into an entire teaching. Temurah transforms a word through structured letter substitutions to uncover new interpretations. Together, these methods teach that language is never just literal — that beneath the words on the page lie patterns, echoes, and deeper structures waiting to be uncovered.

Reading about these techniques reawakened something in me: the sense that the world is full of hidden architecture. That meaning is layered. That the surface is never the whole story. And in that mindset — primed to notice deeper patterns — the golden ratio resurfaced in my life with new resonance.

That recognition sent me down a rabbit hole. I began researching the golden ratio again, not casually but with real curiosity. I looked at how the Fibonacci sequence appears in plant growth, how spirals form in nature, how proportions repeat in art and design, how balance emerges from simple numerical relationships. And the more I learned, the more fascinated I became. It felt like rediscovering a language I had once heard but never fully understood.

And once the idea returned, I began seeing it everywhere.

  • In the spiral of a fern unfurling
  • In the branching of trees
  • In the petal counts of flowers
  • In the way certain spaces feel intuitively balanced without knowing why

It felt less like learning something new and more like remembering something I had always known — a pattern that had been waiting patiently for me to notice it again.

This post is about that rediscovery — the concept itself, the way it re-entered my life, and the rhythm it awakened in how I perceive the world. The practical applications of this pattern — how it shapes garden beds, pathways, color rhythm, furniture placement, and artwork — live in the Garden and Craft pillars, where they naturally belong. But the seed of all of that begins here, with the moment the pattern found me again.
