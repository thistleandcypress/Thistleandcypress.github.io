The garden begins with poor soil and possibility. We’re learning the land as it is — its challenges, its rhythms, its quiet potential — and shaping it into something new. One design choice, one planting, one season at a time, we’re building a landscape that will grow with us: orchard, garden, paths, and the habitats that make this place feel alive.

How We Begin Again

These ten acres ask us to begin with humility. Only a couple of acres are truly open, with the rest held in trees that have been here far longer than we have. It’s not a dramatic wilderness—just a quiet, lived‑in landscape that needs time, attention, and a thoughtful plan. We won’t know exactly what’s possible until the winter snows melt and the ground shows us where the light falls, where the soil drains, and where things naturally want to grow.

For now, we’re learning to observe more than we decide. We walk the property and imagine what might take shape in those open spaces: a small orchard tucked into the sunniest corner, raised beds near the house, native plantings that soften the edges between field and woods. Nothing is fixed yet. Everything is still in conversation.

In the meantime, we’re lucky to have a living example right next door. My sister Holly tends a garden of her own—a space that shifts with the seasons and reminds us what’s possible with patience and care. Her tomatoes, herbs, and summer vegetables offer a glimpse of the future we’re working toward, a gentle encouragement that good soil and good tending can transform a place.

Our goal is simple but meaningful: to have this garden and landscape established by the time I retire in five years. Not finished—gardens never are—but rooted enough to feel like home. A place where fruit trees are settling in, where the soil is waking up again, where the land reflects both the natural character of these acres and the quiet order of a tended garden.

For now, we wait for spring. We make notes. We listen to the land. And we trust that the slow work ahead—steady, thoughtful, and grounded in care—will become its own kind of harvest.
