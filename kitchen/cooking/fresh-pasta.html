Making Fresh Pasta at Home

Understanding flour choice, hydration, and technique — a grounded introduction to pasta-making for beginners.

There's something deeply satisfying about making pasta by hand. It's tactile, forgiving, and far less intimidating than it seems. Once you understand a few fundamentals—flour choice, hydration, and technique—you'll find that fresh pasta becomes less a special occasion and more a reliable kitchen skill.

Flour: The Foundation

Pasta dough comes down to two main flours, each with its own purpose:

Italian 00 Flour

Finely milled from soft wheat, 00 flour creates smooth, silky dough perfect for delicate shapes like tagliatelle, pappardelle, and filled pastas. It has a lower protein content, which makes it tender and easy to roll thin. This is the flour for egg-based doughs and ribbon pastas.

Semolina Flour

Milled from durum wheat, semolina is coarser and higher in protein. It produces a firmer, more textured dough that holds up beautifully in boiling water—ideal for extruded shapes like rigatoni, orecchiette, and cavatelli. Semolina-based doughs are typically made with water rather than eggs, resulting in a chewier, more rustic pasta.

Blending the Two

Many home cooks blend 00 and semolina for an all-purpose dough that balances workability and structure. A ratio of 4 parts 00 flour to 1 part semolina gives you a dough that's easy to roll, holds its shape well, and has a pleasant chew. This blend works beautifully for everyday pasta—fettuccine, lasagne sheets, or simple cut shapes.

Egg or No Egg?

Traditional fresh pasta in Northern Italy (Emilia-Romagna, specifically) uses eggs. Southern Italian pasta often skips the eggs entirely, relying on semolina and water for a firmer, more textured result.

Egg pasta is richer, more tender, and ideal for ribbon shapes and filled pastas. It pairs well with butter, cream, or delicate sauces.

Eggless pasta (semolina and water) is sturdier and better suited for chunky sauces, baked dishes, or shapes that need to hold their form during cooking.

Hydration: Trust Your Hands

Humidity, flour brand, and egg size all affect dough consistency. Rather than rigidly following measurements, pay attention to how the dough feels. It should be smooth, pliable, and slightly tacky—not dry and crumbly, not wet and sticky. If it's too dry, wet your hands and knead in moisture. If too sticky, dust with flour gradually.

This is where pasta-making becomes intuitive. Your hands are your best tool.

Resting: Non-Negotiable

After kneading, pasta dough needs to rest. This allows the gluten to relax, making the dough easier to roll and less likely to snap back. Wrap it tightly in plastic wrap and let it rest at room temperature for at least 30 minutes—up to 2 hours is even better.

Rested dough rolls smoothly, stretches without resistance, and produces pasta with a better texture.

Rolling and Shaping

Whether you're using a rolling pin, a hand-crank pasta machine, or an electric extruder, the goal is the same: consistent thickness and clean cuts.

For ribbon pastas (tagliatelle, fettuccine, pappardelle), roll the dough thin—about 1-2mm—then cut to your desired width.

For filled pastas (ravioli, tortellini, agnolotti), roll even thinner so the filled pockets cook evenly without becoming doughy.

For extruded or hand-shaped pastas (orecchiette, cavatelli, busiate), work with smaller portions of dough and keep the rest covered to prevent drying.

Cooking Fresh Pasta

Fresh pasta cooks fast—usually 2-4 minutes in well-salted boiling water. It's done when it floats and has a slight bite (al dente). Taste a piece to be sure.

Fresh pasta is more delicate than dried, so use a slotted spoon or spider to lift it gently from the water. Reserve a cup of pasta water before draining—it's liquid gold for finishing sauces.
