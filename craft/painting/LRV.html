Understanding Light Reflective Value (LRV)

A practical take from thirty years of painting our own homes

I’m not a professional painter, but I’ve painted every place we’ve lived in over the past thirty-plus years. If you’ve ever stood in front of the paint wall at Lowe’s or Home Depot, you know how overwhelming it can be. Some colors look perfect on the card and turn into disasters on your walls. I’ve learned that the hard way.

Pillar: Craft · Painting · Season: Winter 2026

Painting · Color Selection · Practical Guides

Overview

Every home behaves differently—light, windows, and exposure all change how a color reads.

Light Reflective Value (LRV) is one of the most useful tools for choosing paint colors. It’s a simple number—0 to 100—that tells you how much light a color reflects. Lower numbers absorb light and feel moodier; higher numbers bounce light around and feel brighter. Once you understand LRV, choosing paint becomes far less overwhelming.

What LRV Actually Means

I’d carried a Sherwin-Williams fan deck for years without realizing that the number printed on each color—0 to 100—was the key to why some colors worked beautifully and others fell flat. LRV explains why a color that looks perfect in the store can feel completely wrong at home.

How I Learned This (the Hard Way)

This clicked for me when we lived in Southern California. Our mid-century place there was full of sun, and Skyline Steel (LRV 52) looked fantastic. But when we inherited our Pennsylvania house—with far fewer windows—that same color suddenly made everything feel smaller and heavier.

After a lot of testing, we switched to Shoji White (LRV 74). It’s warm, soft, and brightens the space without feeling like plain white. It made the whole house feel open again.

How to Use LRV When Choosing Paint

  • Higher LRV (70–85): Light, bright, open, airy.
  • Mid-range LRV (45–65): Balanced, versatile, great for most homes.
  • Low LRV (0–35): Moody, dramatic, cozy, saturated.

Trends come and go—right now everyone’s talking about dark, saturated rooms and color-drenching—but it really comes down to what you want your home to feel like.
